The Broken Experience

A clinic that exists, but can't always be relied on

Problem Statement

Students at a campus located far from any city, near Solanpada, depend on a small on-campus clinic for all their medical needs. But the clinic doesn't reliably have a doctor present not just at night, but sometimes during the day too.

When care fails or simply isn't there, the fallback has always been the same: untrained peers improvising first aid, or a long, costly trip to an external town Karjat or Kashele at the exact moment help is needed most.

Phase 01

Empathy & Definition

Before the research

Starting with an assumption and being ready to be wrong

Before talking to anyone, a Proto-Persona was drafted purely from assumption. It was deliberately a guess the plan was always to test it against real accounts and correct it.

Proto-Persona · Assumption-Based, Pre-Research

Rohan, 20, Third-Year Student

Assumed to rely on senior word-of-mouth for clinic hours, hesitant to "make a big deal" out of symptoms, with a vague fallback plan of "ask my roommate" if something went wrong.

  • Doesn't know the clinic's real hours or capabilities
  • Has heard conflicting things about hospital distance and transport
  • No saved emergency numbers, no clear plan for a real crisis
Real interviews

Three real, independent incidents one recurring failure

Three real accounts were gathered from students who experienced medical situations on campus firsthand. Details are anonymized, but every element below is real, not invented.

Interview 01 · Night

The Tile Injury

A sharp, broken floor tile cut a student's leg badly, causing heavy bleeding at night. No doctor was on campus. Other students attempted first aid with no training, and the situation could not be properly resolved on the spot.

Interview 02 · Daytime, Orientation Week

The Panic Attack

An 18-year-old, brand new to college, was alone when a sudden panic attack hit. No doctor was on campus, even during the day. The student was eventually taken nearly an hour away to Karjat.

Interview 03 · Night, then next day

The Food Poisoning

A severe allergic/food-poisoning reaction began at night with no doctor present. The next day, the doctor was available but misdiagnosed it as a stomach ache. The student was later correctly diagnosed at a hospital in Kashele, requiring an overnight stay.

The real user persona

Translating research into a real persona

Built strictly from the three interviews above nothing here is invented.

D

Dhruv

20 · Second-Year Student

Dhruv lives on a remote campus with no hospitals nearby only a campus clinic that doesn't reliably have a doctor present, not just at night, but sometimes during the day too. Over time, he's seen three very different medical situations play out here: a bad tile injury with heavy bleeding at night, a sudden panic attack during orientation week with no one else around, and a food poisoning case that started at night, went unresolved by the doctor even when he was eventually available, and ended in an overnight hospital stay in another town.

Goal

To know, with confidence, that if something goes wrong medically, or in any kind of emergency there's a clear, fast, and reliable way to get real help.

Frustrations
  1. Doctor absence, at both day and night.
  2. Even when the doctor is present, it doesn't guarantee proper care one case was misdiagnosed on campus and only correctly identified after reaching an outside hospital.
  3. Proper care isn't available on campus, which eventually leads to long trips to external towns (Kashele, Karjat).

Core Pain Point · From the lowest dip in the Emotion Line

The moment a student realizes that on-campus medical help isn't available or isn't working and there's no fast, established alternative. That single moment turns a manageable situation into a prolonged, frightening ordeal.

User stories

Three stories, aimed directly at the pain point

Story 01

As a student, I want to securely store my medical information, allergies, and emergency contacts, so that healthcare providers can make informed decisions during emergencies.

Story 02

As a student, I want all emergency contact numbers in one easily accessible place, so that I can quickly reach the right person without searching through multiple sources.

Story 03

As a student, I want to know whether the campus doctor is available and where they can be reached, so that I can decide whether to visit the campus clinic or seek care elsewhere.

Tree test · 5 real participants

Where the V1 structure actually broke down

Each participant was given a real scenario and asked to navigate the V1 tree to the correct destination, without seeing the full structure in advance.

TaskExpected PathSuccess Rate
"Check if the campus clinic is open right now." Medical Services → Clinic Operating Hours 4 / 5
"You suddenly experience severe chest pain and need immediate medical assistance." Emergency → One-Tap SOS 3 / 5
"A friend needs immediate treatment at a nearby hospital." Medical Services → Nearby Hospital Directory 3 / 5
"You want to learn basic first aid before medical help arrives." Resources & Support → First Aid Learning Resources 2 / 5
01
Emergency navigation wasn't fully clear

A 40% failure rate on the chest-pain task, even though the correct destination sat inside Emergency, suggests sub-item ambiguity within the category itself.

02
Hospital information was hard to find under urgency

Participants naturally expected hospital access under Emergency, not under the calmer, browse-oriented Medical Services category a direct mismatch between the task's urgency and its actual placement.

03
First-aid content was the biggest navigation problem

With only a 40% success rate, "Resources & Support" did not clearly signal that first-aid learning material lived inside it.

Phase 03

Prioritization

MoSCoW · MVP scope

Sorting every feature into what actually ships first

With a limited MVP budget, only four features could be Must-Have. Everything else was placed by how directly it addresses the core pain point.

The hardest debate

DFV Matrix settling the final Must-Have slot

The last Must-Have spot came down to two features that, notably, both trace back to the same real incident the food poisoning case. Nearby Hospital Directory gets a student to competent care faster; Allergies & Medical Conditions makes sure whatever care they get is actually informed. Only one could make the cut.

Nearby Hospital Directory

The gap isn't in user need both are genuinely validated. It's in feasibility and viability: Nearby Hospital Directory works for every student from day one, with no data entry and no privacy exposure. Allergies & Medical Conditions only helps if a student has filled in their profile and a provider actually checks it a longer chain with more points of failure.
